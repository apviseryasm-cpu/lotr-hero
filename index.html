Who is the real hero of The Lord of the Rings?

Frodo carried the Ring. Sam carried Frodo. Five serious answers exist, all of them defensible, and which one you find obvious says a lot about how you met the story.

This is one of those arguments that never resolves, because everyone is answering a slightly different question. Who did the most good? Who suffered most? Who could the story not survive without? Pick a different one of those and you get a different hero.

So before you decide, here is the strongest version of each case — including the ones people usually leave out.

The five real answers

Frodo Baggins

He is the one who actually did it, in the plainest sense: he carried the thing. For seventeen quiet years in the Shire first, and then — when it mattered — into Mordor, with the Ring getting heavier and more persuasive every mile, while it slowly ate his memory of the Shire and his ability to be a person.

The usual objection is that he failed at the end — he claimed the Ring on the slopes of Mount Doom rather than throwing it in. But that is arguably the point rather than a flaw: nobody could have done it. Frodo got the Ring further than any being in Middle-earth's history, and the failure at the last step was the failure everyone would have had.

Samwise Gamgee

The case for Sam is emotional, and it is very hard to argue with. He is the only person who goes the entire distance for no reason other than love, with no destiny, no prophecy and no crown waiting at the end. He carries Frodo physically up the mountain when Frodo's legs stop working.

The detail people raise most is the Ring itself. Sam takes it, briefly, on the pass of Cirith Ungol — and then he gives it back. He is the only person in the story who picks the Ring up and voluntarily puts it down again. If the test of a hero is what you do when the worst thing in the world is in your hand, that is a short and devastating argument.

Aragorn

Without Aragorn, the Ring never reaches Mordor at all — because nothing distracts Sauron. He is the one who unites Rohan and Gondor, walks the Paths of the Dead, and then marches an army to the Black Gate on a deliberate suicide mission to draw Sauron's eye away from two hobbits crawling up a mountain.

He is also the only major character whose arc is about accepting greatness rather than resisting temptation — and the films lean on that heavily, handing him the crowning, the charge and the big speeches. Which may be why he comes up so often from people who met the story on screen.

Gandalf

Nothing happens without him. He works out what the Ring is, he is the one who insists it cannot be used, he survives Moria, and he spends the rest of the story arranging everyone else's courage — including, famously, letting others make the decisions rather than making them himself.

The counter-argument is that Gandalf is a Maia sent back by higher authority, so his heroism is more like a job than a choice. Which is a fair objection and also exactly the kind of thing that makes people pick him anyway.

Gollum

The answer nobody wants, and the one the book arguably forces on you. Frodo could not destroy the Ring. Nobody could. It goes into the fire because Gollum dances on the edge of the Crack of Doom in triumph, and slips.

And the reason Gollum is there is Frodo's mercy — twice over. If Frodo had killed him when he had every reason to, the Ring survives and Sauron wins. On that reading the destruction of the Ring is not an act of will at all. It is the delayed consequence of a kindness.

Why people disagree — and why you can often guess their answer

There is a fairly reliable pattern in this argument, and it follows how you met the story.

The books spend a great deal of time inside Sam's head. You get his fear, his homesickness, his bewilderment at Frodo's distance, and his stubbornness. Readers who came to the story that way tend to see the emotional centre of it sitting with him — and the ending lands differently, because the last stretch belongs to Sam in a way it doesn't quite on screen.

The films give their big moments to Frodo and to Aragorn: the scope, the sacrifices, the crowning. Viewers who met Middle-earth on screen are often answering a question about who the lead was, which is a subtly different question from who the hero was.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding. It is the reason the tally below is split by how you came to the story — book first, film first, or both together. If the pattern is real, it will show up. If it isn't, the tally will say so, and that is just as interesting.

Still undecided?

Then answer the question this way instead: if you removed exactly one person from the story and everything else stayed as it was, whose removal loses you the Ring?

It is Frodo, and it is Sam, and it is Gollum — which is probably why this argument has run for seventy years and will run for seventy more.
